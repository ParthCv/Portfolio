Part One — World & Lore
Section 01
The World Above

The game is set in Harwick — a fictional mid-sized North American city. Not glamorous enough to be a tourist destination, not small enough for everyone to know each other. The kind of city where a person can disappear into debt, bad decisions, and a basement apartment, and nobody bats an eye.

Harwick has a working port, a downtown financial district, a cluster of residential neighbourhoods, and an economy that was "revitalising" a decade ago and never quite finished. It feels slightly overcast even on sunny days. Good for crime. Bad for morale.

The police department is understaffed and overstretched. The courts process petty criminals on a conveyor belt. There is no dramatic crime war going on — just the quiet, unglamorous machinery of people doing illegal things and mostly getting away with it, until they don't.

Section 02
The Backrooms — A History

The Backrooms are not a glitch. They are not a simulation. They are not hell, though some of what lives there might make you reconsider.

The current working theory among those who study them — a very small, very quiet group — is that the Backrooms are a spatial bleed: a layer of reality that exists underneath, alongside, or inside the physical world, accessible only through anomalous structural failures in space. These failures are called no-clips. You fall through a wall, a floor, a threshold that looks solid and isn't, and you're in.

No-clips have always existed. Medieval accounts describe monks walking through monastery walls into "corridors of yellow silence." A 1947 industrial accident in Ohio saw fourteen workers vanish from a basement — three returned, all mute, over the following weeks. A leaked 1983 Soviet report documented a facility that "partially intersected with non-standard spatial topology." The government suppressed all of it.

DECLASSIFIED // PROJECT STILLWATER // DOC-114b

"...anomalous zone first confirmed navigable by Agent XXXXXXX in 1991. Zone interior demonstrates non-Euclidean geometry inconsistent with any known physical model. Agent reported ambient fluorescent lighting, carpeted floors, persistent smell of damp. No external light source identified. Duration of stay: approx. 14 hours subjective / 22 minutes objective..."

"...entities encountered on second entry. Classification: REDACTED. Recommend all further entries be conducted with REDACTED..."

STATUS: REDACTED // CLEARANCE LEVEL 6+ ONLY

By the 2000s, a classified US programme had a working understanding of the Backrooms. They had mapped roughly forty stable entry points nationally, lost eleven field operatives, and learned that the Backrooms were vast, partially navigable, extremely dangerous, and — crucially — not useful enough to justify the casualties. The programme was quietly mothballed.

What the government did not anticipate was the internet.

Section 03
The Underground Discovers It

In the mid-2000s, grainy footage of "liminal spaces" began circulating on early internet forums. Most was photographers and urban explorers. But buried in the noise were posts describing something different — places accessed by accident, with no visible entry or exit, with the specific texture of wrongness the Backrooms generate.

By 2015, a loose network was deliberately attempting access. Most failed. Some succeeded and didn't come back. A few survived and started talking — carefully, pseudonymously, on dark web forums with strict vetting. Organised crime found this network the way it finds everything useful: through someone who owed money.

forum: LIMINAL_OPS // thread: it's_not_local // user: wallcrawler_88 // [archived]
ok i need someone to check my work because i think i've been wrong about everything for two years.

i marked my exit point. measured it. 400m east of my entry, every time, consistent. so i assumed the corridors mapped to real space. local bleed. made sense.

last week i went deeper than i ever have. took a wrong turn, clipped out to test my bearings. the street signs were in a language i don't read. the cars were on the wrong side. i was not on my continent.

the 400m thing was never real. it was just the shallowest exit and i never went past it. the backrooms don't map to here. they map to EVERYWHERE. every point on earth is one wrong turn away if you go deep enough.

do you understand what this means. there is no bank on this planet you can't reach from your own basement.

i'm not posting again. good luck.

By the early 2020s, at least three major criminal organisations in North America were running Backrooms logistics networks (detailed in Section 08). None trust each other. They coexist inside the same anomaly because it is big enough that you can go a very long time without running into anyone who wants to kill you. Usually.

Section 04b
Global Topology — Two Layers

The single most important rule of this world: the Backrooms warp space. Distance in the physical world means nothing inside. A wall in your Harwick basement does not connect to "somewhere 380m east" — it connects to wherever the topology folds it, and the topology folds the entire planet into a single navigable interior. The bank you crack tonight might bleed in from Seoul; tomorrow's from Zürich, Mumbai, São Paulo. You never travel across the world — you travel through the Backrooms, which touch everywhere at once.

The world is built from two layers — one that stays still so the player can learn it, one that reshuffles so every day feels fresh:

Difficulty is measured in depth, not real-world distance — how far into the Backrooms a target sits. Higher-value targets bleed in deeper, behind heavier entity presence, with harder cracks. A suburban credit union sits shallow; a Swiss private bank sits deep.

Why this is the whole pitch

"You are under house arrest in a basement in a forgettable city. You cannot leave your home. And you are robbing banks on six continents. The contradiction IS the fantasy."

Production Reality
Global Lore, Single Asset

The world is global. The build is not. The global scope lives entirely in the job briefing ("Target: private bank, Seoul financial district") — flavour and difficulty signal, delivered as text and audio, never as a 3D environment. When you reach a target you clip through one wall into a single self-contained room: the vault interior. You are never in "the bank," only ever inside the one room that holds the loot. Every vault reuses the same core safe-room asset, lightly redressed by type and value tier. The Backrooms themselves are the real explorable space; targets are pocket dimensions you punch into and out of.

In-world justification

"Why do all vaults feel the same? Because you're not seeing the bank — you're seeing the bleed. The Backrooms render every vault through the same anomalous geometry. A vault in Delhi and a vault in Oslo arrive at your wall wearing the same face. The world flattens itself to fit the corridor."

Section 05
Entities — What Lives There
Status — parked for later

Entities are the heaviest system in the game: each needs behaviour, a tell, an avoidance pattern, and integration with the procedural map. They are deliberately deferred until the core skeleton is proven. What follows is the design philosophy to build against when the time comes — not a finished spec.

The Backrooms are not empty. Entities do not hunt in the conventional sense — they respond: to sound, to panic, to prolonged presence in their zone. The core doctrine: if you study them, you can avoid them. If you panic, you die.

Each entity has a tell — an audio or environmental cue signalling proximity — and a response pattern the player can learn and exploit. None chase indefinitely. All are patient. Entities do not spawn near stable entry/exit points, trader hubs, or the safe room behind a stealing door; they occupy the deep transit corridors between you and the target. Gameplay against them is stealth, hiding, and observation — never combat. The player's real weapon is knowledge, accumulated in the laptop wiki (Section 09.6).

Design philosophy (hold this when building entities)

"Entities are not jump-scare delivery mechanisms. They are environmental hazards with learnable behaviour. Every entity kill should feel like the player's fault — because it was."

Section 06
The Protagonist & Cast
The Player Character — "The Clipper"
// small-time thief, recently released, accidentally brilliant
Not a mastermind, not especially brave. Got into theft through proximity and a group of friends who turned out not to be friends. Served time for a robbery that wasn't entirely their idea and entirely their fault to get caught in. Released on probation with a house-arrest monitor and a basement flat. Their defining trait is adaptability — not a planner, but the kind of person who treats impossible situations as logistics problems rather than existential ones. The Backrooms don't break them the way they break most people; they're too busy thinking about the route home.
Razor — "The Friend"
// fixer, tech guy, running partner, potential liability
Wasn't at the original job — the one guy smart enough not to show up. Longer in the life, more connections, a moral compass pointing roughly toward "we" rather than "me," most of the time. Handles the tech: tracers, receivers, dark web research, the laptop. Found the forum posts. Takes a cut of everything. Genuinely funny — texts during heists, has opinions about your technique — and will absolutely sell you out if pushed far enough. The game tracks how far that is.
Gravel — "The Trader"
// black market fence, operates from The Hub, neutral not harmless
Nobody knows how long he's been in The Hub or how he got there, and he doesn't offer this. Sells equipment, buys stolen goods, extends credit to reliable operators at unkind-but-not-cartoonish rates. Speaks in the flat cadence of someone who has heard every story and rated most of them mediocre. Not an ally — a recurring business contact who happens to exist in a dimension most people don't know about.
Section 07
The Discovery — Opening Lore

It starts with the wall in the basement. Three weeks after release, moving boxes and ignoring a parole officer's call, the protagonist leans against the far wall and goes through it. Not dramatically — they're just in a corridor that smells like carpet adhesive and hasn't seen sunlight since before they were born, and the wall behind them looks perfectly solid.

They panic, stumble back through a seam to the basement floor, and tell Razor. Razor doesn't think they've lost it — he thinks they've found something. Three days of dark web research later: the forums, the archived posts, the cautious documentation of levels and entities, and wallcrawler_88's revelation that distance was always fake.

Razor does the maths. The ankle monitor that traps them in a basement is meaningless — the whole planet is reachable through one wall. They are the most mobile criminal on Earth and they never have to leave home. They look at the monitor. They look at the laptop. They look at each other.

Tone anchor

"The game begins not with urgency but with two idiots who have just realised they might have found the world's greatest criminal loophole and are trying very hard not to get excited about it."

Section 08
Criminal Factions
The Caldera Cartel
// encountered via: porter / supply runs
Drug and weapons cartel with established Backrooms logistics. Uses the clipper population as independent couriers — plausible deniability, no direct connection. Porter missions are well-paid, high-risk, and come with a clear message: lose the cargo and you pay for it. Not cruel without reason. Very cruel with reason.
Seraya Lenta — "Grey Ribbon"
// encountered via: advanced / late content
Russian syndicate that moves people through the Backrooms; most of those people did not choose to go. Encountering their operations is a deliberate tone shift — the dark-comedy register drops entirely. They are the hard ceiling of how bad things get in this world.
The Independent Network
// no name — where the protagonist belongs
No hierarchy, no membership — just reputation, trust, and the shared interest of everyone in The Hub in not making enemies. The protagonist earns standing through successful runs, honoured debts, and not getting anyone killed. Standing unlocks better contracts, better traders, and information about what's deeper in.
The Government — Project Stillwater remnants
// background presence, encountered late game
The programme was mothballed; the knowledge wasn't destroyed. Someone in a federal agency has watched the clipper community grow for a decade, waiting for a reason to re-engage. The protagonist's escalating profile may become that reason. Their interest isn't criminal justice — it's strategic. They want back in.
Part Two — Core Systems
Section 09
The Core Loop

Every day runs the same skeleton so the player internalises the rhythm; the content inside reshuffles daily so it never goes stale.

1
Wake up
New day, bleed regenerated.
2
Check the board
Razor has posted 1–3 jobs to the laptop, tagged value / location / depth. You choose.
3
Gear up & clip in
Pack inventory — tools, consumables, empty loot space. Step through the basement wall.
4
Navigate the bleed
Find the target's corridor via tracer. Read entity tells. The longest, tensest stretch.
5
Clip into the safe
Punch through one wall. Run the cracking minigame against a clock.
6
Grab & get out
Loot into limited inventory. Retrace the route home.
7
Sell & launder
At the Hub: cash to the launderer, valuables to the fence. Both convert to safe bank money.
8
Pay Razor
Drop his cut. Skip too often and his trust drops — eventually he turns on you.
9
Sleep
Spend on gear via the laptop, then sleep to advance. The day resets.

09.3 — Time, night, and why you sleep

Time passes as you operate, and the Backrooms worsen as it gets later: entity spawn rate climbs, jumpscares get more frequent, and liminal randomness spikes — the Kane Pixels texture of the space becoming visibly unstable (a crashed car half-merged into a wall, a chair embedded in the ceiling, the cardboard cutout figure watching, balloons drifting a dead corridor, lights stuttering). Sleeping is the safe reset. But night is also when rare anomalous objects spawn — high-value oddities that only appear when the space destabilises. So night becomes a real choice: sleep safe, or push deeper for the rare spawn and risk losing everything you carry.

09.4 — Inventory & carry-risk

You cannot carry everything; loot, tools, and consumables compete for limited space. The critical tension is the death penalty on unsold goods: loot in your inventory is unbanked and at risk — die to an entity on the way home and you lose what you carry. Selling or laundering at the Hub converts it to safe bank money, which death cannot touch. Every run carries an escalating "cash out or push on" decision.

The loop's central tension

"Everything you're carrying is at risk until you sell it. Greed is the real enemy — the entities are just how greed kills you."

09.5 — Progression philosophy

Guiding principle: tools make the mundane easy and the impossible possible. Most gear lowers friction on routine tasks (friction-reducers: better tracer, minigame helpers, energy drinks). A smaller set acts as keys unlocking specific high-tier jobs or regions (e.g. a mask for the Pool Rooms, entity counters, deep-corridor equipment). Every purchase must answer a clear "this lets me do X." No stat bloat.

09.6 — The laptop wiki

Entity gameplay is stealth and reading signs — the player's real weapon is knowledge, and the laptop wiki is where it accumulates. Survive an entity, observe its tell, and the wiki fills in. A new player dies to the unknown; a veteran reads the room. A fresh entity drops them back into fear. The laptop also holds the job board, bank account, and gear shop.

Section 10
The Tracer & The Find

The tracer's defining choice is what it does not do: it does not route you. It gives a heading — a raw direction toward the target, pointing straight through walls — and leaves the maze-solving to you. You track one job at a time. The gap between "the direction I want" and "the path the maze allows" is the entire navigation game. The player is never lost, but never guided.

Core principle

"A GPS draws your path and removes the maze. A compass only points and keeps every corridor a decision. Never lost, never guided — the sweet spot where a maze stays tense instead of frustrating."

The find runs four beats, escalating from broad navigation to a tight, tactile search:

1
Lock the job
Tracer locks a heading toward the target's bleed point.
2
Follow the heading
Travel toward the direction through the maze — wrong turns, dead ends, wrap-arounds.
3
Proximity glow
Close to the room, the tracer lights up. You're in the right area — now find the exact wall.
4
Find the seam
Push against walls to find where the geometry gives. Clip through into the safe.

Once found, you mark the no-clip door with blue tape (the film nod). A taped door is a known door — it persists so you can return, under two conditions, whichever comes first: until you sleep (the bleed regenerates) or until the room is empty. This unlocks multi-trip looting: a rich room you can't empty in one inventory load forces the cash-out-or-push-on choice again and again. The blue tape is the greed engine.

The room behind the stealing door is a safe area — entities don't follow in, linger, or spawn there. A deliberate pressure-release valve and a reliable anchor for multi-trip looting: the destination is always safe even when the journey never is.

The tracer always knows the way to home base (always tracked) and to the launderer and fence (tracked after first found) — the fixed stars of the constant layer. The heading home is always available: the player can never be truly lost, only in danger.

Section 11
Vault Cracking

The minigame is the climax of a run — a skill check under a clock, the only thing between the player and the money. Tonally the inverse of the corridor: out there you were quiet and hiding; in here you're fast, working with your hands against a countdown. The safe room is entity-free, so the threat is the alarm — start the crack and the clock starts. Finish in time or you're caught: hauled out, fined, dumped home.

A vault crack is not one minigame — it's a sequence of small timed tasks sharing one countdown. Build a handful of good task types; generate variety by combining and parameterising. The tier scales four dials at once:

Tools either reduce friction on tasks you can do or are keys unlocking task types — and therefore vaults — you can't attempt without them. This powers multi-vault jobs: a single job can hold multiple vaults at different security levels. The first you crack today; the second is sealed behind a tool you haven't bought, becoming a reason to come back stronger.

Variety without new content comes from three axes: combination (same tasks, different sequences under one clock), parameterisation (tunable pin count, code length, timer, tolerance), and unlock cadence (new task types arrive with new tools, growing the palette as the old one wears).

Section 12
Supply Runs

The courier layer — you're not stealing, you're carrying. Deliberately simpler than a heist: no minigame, no crack. The entire challenge is the journey, twice over.

1
Accept the run
Take a supply contract instead of (or alongside) a theft job.
2
Find the pickup
Tracer points to the pickup room. Navigate, find the seam, clip in.
3
Grab the cargo
Pick the goods off the floor. No skill check — just load it.
4
Get the dropoff
Destination appears on the tracer. Carry the contraband across the map.
5
Place the goods
Deliver the correct cargo. Done right, you're paid. Done wrong, you pay.

Supply failure — wrong cargo, undelivered, or dying mid-run — pulls money straight out of your bank, the one mechanic that breaks the "banked money is safe" rule. That makes supply runs uniquely scary: you're risking savings, not just today's haul.

Behind every supplier is a hidden relationship meter — a reputation system. Deliver well and it climbs (higher pay, more frequent and lucrative contracts); fail and it drops (reduced pay, fewer jobs, eventually a supplier who stops trusting you). This turns supply runs into a long-game investment in who you work for as more suppliers come online.

Part Three — Synthesis
Systems Map
The Whole Machine

Steps 1–6 form a closed flywheel: take a job → navigate → crack/deliver → sell & bank → buy gear → unlock harder jobs → which loops back. That closed loop is the engine of the entire game; everything else modifies it. The lower bands are the systems that shape the flywheel without being part of it; the bottom band is the constant layer that never moves.

The one balance risk to watch

The flywheel is healthy — money buys capability, capability unlocks harder content, harder content pays more. The risk: if gear makes things too easy too fast, the loop spins out and the horror evaporates. Keep gear unlocking access to harder things rather than trivialising existing things (the "keys over stat-boosts" rule). Hold that line and the loop self-balances.

New crime types to plug into the job framework: exam theft (the funny one), arson, painting heists for clients, stealing documents from a CEO, smuggling people, etc. Needs a design pass on how each maps onto the existing job / supply-run structures.
Caught vs killed — penalty separationdecide
"Caught" (fail minigame → fine, scales with tier) and "killed" (entity → lose carried loot + fine) should feel different.

Two readable options: (A) night-only rare anomalous spawns — push-your-luck; (B) a flat payout multiplier for operating late.

Wall-search feedback modellock before build
Bumping walls to find the seam dies without escalating feedback. Needs a hot/cold signal — tracer pulsing faster, rising audio tone, or a faint shimmer on the right stretch of wall.
Recommendation: lock a feedback model before this beat is prototyped.
Safe room clockdecide
Confirm the day/night clock keeps ticking while inside the safe room, so camping costs a dangerous night walk home (anti-camp guardrail).
Recommendation: yes — keep the clock running inside.
Procgen connectivity guaranteelock before build
The "wrong turn, wrap around" fantasy only works if the map has loops and alternate routes, not dead-end trees. From anywhere, there must be more than one way to anywhere. A hard requirement on the map generator from day one.
2D / 3D minigame ratiolock before build
A real budget fork. 3D diegetic tasks (drill, acid cut) are the immersive "wow" but expensive (physics, rigs, animation); 2D overlays are fast and easy to balance.
Recommendation: ship a core of 3–4 solid 2D overlays first, reserve 1–2 hero 3D tasks for high tiers. Don't make everything 3D.
Tool-gated "come back later" consistencylock before build

Recommendation: tool-gated content reappears via the daily job rotation — "come back later" means "when this job type spawns again, geared up," not "preserve this exact door."
Supply failure draining bank moneydecide
The one mechanic that breaks "banked money is safe." Confirm it's intentional, and decide whether the penalty is capped or can drive you negative (which feeds a debt spiral with Razor / the cartel).
Unified relationship frameworkdecide
Razor's trust, supplier rep, and Independent Network standing are all the same kind of system. Worth treating as one "standing" framework under the hood for consistency and a single laptop view.

One job at a time for now. A future option: let veterans see all taken jobs and plan an efficient route between them. Adds skill expression but also UI and balancing complexity. Revisit when jobs-per-day is finalised.

Next Steps
Where This Goes Next

The skeleton is complete: world, loop, navigation, vaults, supply runs, economy, relationships, fail states. The remaining big pieces (entities, endgame, crime catalogue) all hang off this skeleton rather than adding new structural machinery.

Your stated next directions, with how this doc supports them:

MVP planning
The flywheel (systems map, steps 1–6) is the MVP target — one job tier, one safe-room asset, the tracer, a couple of 2D minigame tasks, sell/bank, sleep. Prove the loop is fun before layering entities or supply runs.
Art direction
Two visual registers to nail: the constant Backrooms (Kane Pixels liminal — yellow rooms, fluorescent dread) and the night-time instability (glitch anomalies). The safe-room asset is the single most-reused art piece — invest there.
Platform & tech
Key technical pillars from the TBD register: procgen with a connectivity guarantee, the heading-only tracer, blue-tape door persistence, and the 2D/3D minigame split. These shape the engine choice.
